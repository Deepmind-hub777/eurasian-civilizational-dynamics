# Eurasian Civilizational Dynamics — Initial Research Proposal

**Project title (Traditional Chinese):** 歐亞文明歷史動力學  
**Project code:** ECD  
**Version:** v0.1 — Initial Research Proposal  
**Type:** Open, interdisciplinary, evidence-based historical research  
**Shared research languages:** Traditional Chinese and English

---

## 1. Background and Rationale

The history of Eurasia did not unfold as a collection of isolated civilizations. Empires organized territory and institutions; commercial networks connected cities and markets; migrations reshaped populations and social structures; religions and ideas crossed political boundaries; and knowledge, technologies, and material goods moved along multiple routes.

Yet historical narratives often take empires, dynasties, or nation-states as their primary units of analysis. This can obscure interactions that crossed political borders. When an empire declined or a political center shifted, some networks persisted, migrated, reorganized, or broke down. These different outcomes require investigation through concrete evidence and comparative research.

The project is organized around one central question:

> **How does civilization continue to move between the rise and fall of empires?**

The project does not assume that networks are necessarily more resilient than empires, nor that every instance of cross-regional contact represents direct transmission. Its goal is to develop historical explanations that can be tested, revised, and challenged.

## 2. Research Objectives

1. Establish a synchronized Eurasian historical framework from the 2nd century BCE to the 5th century CE.
2. Compare political and social developments across East, Central, South, and West Asia, the Mediterranean, and the steppe.
3. Reconstruct commercial, migratory, religious, and knowledge networks, including their nodes, routes, and interactions.
4. Examine network persistence, migration, reorganization, and rupture during shifts in political power.
5. Build a layered evidence base that keeps primary sources, scholarly interpretations, hypotheses, and synthesis models traceable.
6. Produce research that can be openly reviewed and continuously revised through interdisciplinary collaboration.

## 3. Scope and Chronological Framework

### 3.1 Core Comparative Period

**2nd century BCE to 5th century CE.**

This period includes the Han imperial order and its neighbors, steppe political confederations, Central Asian commerce, the Roman Empire and Mediterranean world, and the development of diverse religious and intellectual traditions. Chronological slices will be defined according to specific research questions rather than forcing every region into one political timeline.

### 3.2 Longitudinal Extensions

**Selected transregional networks from the 1st to 14th centuries CE.**

These case studies trace particular networks over longer periods, especially Christianity and the Church of the East across Central Asia, Persia, India, and China. Such extensions do not redefine the project's core comparative period.

### 3.3 Geographic Scope

East Asia, Central Asia, South Asia, West Asia, the Mediterranean, Europe, and the Eurasian steppe. Geographic boundaries will be defined by the research question; modern national borders will not be treated as equivalent to ancient cultural boundaries.

## 4. Principal Research Questions

- How did empires organize territory, transport, taxation, military power, and transregional order?
- How did merchants, migrants, missionaries, diplomats, and knowledge communities establish connections across regions?
- How did religious networks spread, localize, and reorganize across different languages, regimes, and social systems?
- Which networks persisted, migrated, reorganized, or ruptured when political centers declined or shifted?
- How did node autonomy, connectivity, trust, and adaptive capacity affect the historical resilience of networks?
- How do gaps, biases, and preservation conditions in surviving evidence constrain the reconstruction of ancient networks?

## 5. Method I: Trans-Civilizational Historical Slicing (TCHS)

TCHS is a proposed comparative working method with three complementary dimensions:

1. **Synchronicity:** Place different regions on shared chronological coordinates, distinguishing contemporary events from sequential events and later retrospection.
2. **Multidimensionality:** Examine politics, population, economy, religion, thought, technology, environment, and institutions together.
3. **Multi-scalar analysis:** Move between individuals, households, cities, networks, and empires.

A historical slice is not intended to reduce complex history to a static snapshot. It creates a defined time window in which multiple regions and dimensions can be compared; successive slices then reveal change over time.

## 6. Method II: Decentralized Self-Organizing Networks (DSON)

DSON is a hypothesis to be tested: civilizational continuity and transformation may depend not only on imperial centers, but also on the connectivity, transmission, adaptation, and reorganization of transregional networks.

Preliminary analytical variables:

- **N — Node Autonomy**
- **C — Connectivity and Redundancy**
- **T — Trust and Norms**
- **F — Flows of people, goods, information, and knowledge**
- **R — Resilience**
- **A — Adaptation and Reorganization**

Decentralization does not mean the absence of centrality. A network may contain highly central hubs without being wholly controlled by a single authority. Hubs can improve efficiency while also creating single points of failure. The project will compare observed outcomes rather than assume a predetermined conclusion.

## 8. Research Volumes

1. **Volume I — Empires and the Steppe:** Power structures, frontier orders, alliances, and political transitions.
2. **Volume II — Commerce and Transport:** Merchants, cities, routes, commodities, information, and technology flows.
3. **Volume III — Faith and Thought:** Religious communities, missionary routes, texts, and intellectual exchange.
4. **Volume IV — Institutions and Civilization:** Political institutions, social organization, and transformations from antiquity to the medieval period.

DSON is a cross-cutting analytical framework, not a fifth volume.

## 9. Evidence and Source Standards

Research materials are organized into four layers:

- **Layer A — Primary Evidence:** Manuscripts, inscriptions, archaeological finds, contemporary documents, and other identifiable primary materials.
- **Layer B — Scholarly Interpretation:** Editions, translations, dating arguments, archaeological reports, and peer-reviewed scholarship.
- **Layer C — Historical Hypotheses:** Explicit statements of supporting evidence, counter-evidence, competing explanations, and confidence.
- **Layer D — Synthesis Models:** Timelines, maps, network diagrams, and civilizational-dynamics models.

Layer D models must never overwrite Layer A evidence. Damaged text, editorial restorations, variant readings, and disputed dates must retain their uncertainty markers.

## 10. Research Principles

1. Distinguish source statements, scholarly inference, and project hypotheses.
2. Do not treat chronological sequence as proof of causation.
3. Similar ethnonyms do not by themselves establish identical populations or political communities.
4. Contemporary existence does not by itself prove direct contact between networks.
5. Network persistence does not prove uninterrupted institutional continuity.
6. Absence of surviving evidence is not proof that an event did not occur.
7. Do not impose one civilization's conceptual categories on another's historical context.
8. Theological interpretation and historical evidence analysis may be presented together, but their distinct forms of reasoning must remain explicit.

## 11. Open Collaboration

Researchers and members of the public from different disciplines are welcome. Collaboration will take place through public documents, GitHub Issues, Pull Requests, source annotations, and version history.

Important historical claims should, where possible, include:
- Source title, author/editor, publication or collection details;
- Page, section, inscription line, or archival identifier;
- Original text, transliteration, translation, and variant readings;
- Evidence category and uncertainty;
- Potential counter-evidence and alternative explanations.

## 12. Expected Outputs

- A traceable synchronized Eurasian chronology;
- Structured records of people, places, events, and network nodes;
- Case studies of commercial, migratory, religious, and knowledge networks;
- Multilingual primary-source and scholarly bibliography indexes;
- Visual maps, timelines, and network models;
- Research papers and reports subject to open review and version control.

## 13. Central Proposition to Be Tested

> The continuity and transformation of Eurasian civilizations cannot be understood solely through the rise and fall of imperial centers; the connections, flows, adaptation, and reorganization of transregional networks may also be essential to explaining civilizational change.

This proposition is a starting point, not a predetermined conclusion. Research should be capable of supporting, revising, or falsifying it.
