# Trans-Civilizational Historical Slicing (TCHS)

**Status:** ECD working method v0.1; subject to case testing and revision.

## 1. Definition

Trans-Civilizational Historical Slicing (TCHS) is a comparative research design. Within a clearly defined time window, it places different civilizational regions on shared chronological coordinates and examines their contemporaneous developments, interactions, and differences across multiple historical dimensions and scales.

It does not treat civilizations as closed, homogeneous entities, nor does it replace regional chronologies with a single imperial timeline.

## 2. Three Dimensions

### 2.1 Synchronicity

- State event dates, date ranges, and the basis for dating.
- Distinguish the time of an event, the time it was recorded, the time a text was compiled, and later retrospective accounts.
- Explain calendar systems, conversions, and disputed chronologies.
- Contemporaneity establishes temporal coexistence, not causation or direct contact.

### 2.2 Multidimensionality

Depending on the available evidence, a slice may examine:
- Politics and warfare
- Population, migration, and intergroup relations
- Trade, agriculture, finance, and transport
- Religion, thought, and education
- Technology, craft, and knowledge transmission
- Environment, climate, and disease
- Law, institutions, and social organization

A case is not required to cover every dimension. Missing evidence must be marked explicitly.

### 2.3 Multi-scalar Analysis

Move between:
- Individuals and households
- Settlements, cities, and local communities
- Commercial, religious, migratory, and knowledge networks
- Kingdoms, empires, and transregional political systems

Macro-level models must remain testable against concrete people, places, texts, and archaeological evidence.

## 3. Workflow

1. **Define the question:** Identify the phenomenon to explain without presupposing the answer.
2. **Set the time window:** Choose a year, short interval, or broader period and explain why.
3. **Set the geographic scope:** Identify included and excluded regions.
4. **Build a synchronization table:** List events, dating evidence, and uncertainty.
5. **Organize dimensions:** Classify material by politics, population, economy, religion, technology, and other relevant domains.
6. **Identify actors and networks:** Record people, institutions, cities, routes, and demonstrable links.
7. **Compare:** Explain similarities, differences, possible interactions, and unverified connections.
8. **Test counter-evidence:** Examine alternative explanations, chronological conflicts, and source biases.
9. **Connect adjacent slices:** Track change over time; do not mistake one cross-section for a complete history.

## 4. Minimum Output Fields

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Slice ID | Unique identifier |
| Research question | Central question |
| Time window | Period and dating basis |
| Regions | Geographic coverage |
| Dimensions | Historical dimensions used |
| Actors and nodes | People, groups, cities, institutions |
| Events and relations | Events and evidence-supported relations |
| Evidence references | Sources and precise locations |
| Uncertainties | Uncertainty and evidence gaps |
| Alternative explanations | Competing accounts |
| Revision history | Change record |

## 5. Common Errors

- Converting regional dates without explaining the basis.
- Treating later accounts as contemporary records.
- Mistaking contemporaneity for direct contact.
- Explaining similar cultural phenomena as proof of one-way transmission.
- Imposing modern ethnic or national categories on ancient societies.
- Selecting only evidence that supports a preferred hypothesis.

## 6. Limitations

TCHS is a research design, not an automatic causal explanation. Causal claims still require examination of mechanisms, chronology, source quality, alternative explanations, and counter-evidence. Results are also affected by uneven source survival, language access, and researcher selection.

## 7. Relationship to DSON

TCHS establishes comparable historical slices. DSON may then be used as a hypothesis framework for analyzing network structures and change within those slices. They are distinct: the method specifies how to observe; the hypothesis proposes an explanation that can be tested.
