# Decentralized Self-Organizing Networks (DSON)

**Status:** ECD preliminary analytical hypothesis v0.1; not an established law of history.

## 1. Core Hypothesis

Civilizational continuity and transformation may depend not only on the stability of imperial centers, but also on the connectivity, flows of information and resources, trust norms, adaptive capacity, and reorganization of transregional networks.

The hypothesis asks whether different networks maintained some of their functions when political centers shifted. If so, through which nodes, relationships, and institutions? If not, what caused disruption?

## 2. Definitions

- **Decentralization:** A network is not wholly controlled by a single center. This does not mean it lacks hubs or power inequalities.
- **Self-organization:** Actors develop observable patterns of coordination through repeated interaction, local norms, and distributed decisions. This does not imply an absence of institutions.
- **Network:** A set of identifiable nodes and relations. Nodes may include individuals, households, cities, communities, institutions, or transport centers.
- **Resilience:** The capacity of a network to maintain, recover, or adapt specified functions after disruption. The function and evidence must be stated.

## 3. Six Preliminary Analytical Variables

| Code | Variable | Research focus |
|---|---|---|
| N | Node Autonomy | Whether nodes can operate when a center fails or power changes |
| C | Connectivity and Redundancy | Multiple routes, alternative nodes, and transregional links |
| T | Trust and Norms | Shared rules, reputation, commitments, and reciprocity |
| F | Flows | Movement of people, goods, information, capital, and knowledge |
| R | Resilience | Which functions persist, recover, or disappear after disruption |
| A | Adaptation and Reorganization | Changes in routes, nodes, rules, or organizational forms |

These are analytical tools. They should not be converted into apparently precise scores where evidence does not support measurement.

## 4. Principal Network Types

1. **Commercial networks:** Merchant families, caravans, cities, ports, markets, and credit relations.
2. **Religious networks:** Missionaries, local communities, churches, bishops, monasteries, texts, and liturgies.
3. **Migration networks:** Population movements, kinship ties, political alliances, settlement, and admixture.
4. **Knowledge and information networks:** Documents, translation, scholarly transmission, technologies, and communication; these may be analyzed as a cross-cutting layer.

Networks may overlap, but overlap must be supported by specific evidence. Shared geography or chronology alone is insufficient.

## 5. Comparable Historical Outcomes

Research should distinguish at least the following:
- **Persistence:** Core functions and relationships remain identifiable after disruption.
- **Partial persistence:** Some nodes or functions remain while others disappear.
- **Migration:** The network's center of gravity or routes shift to another region.
- **Reorganization:** Nodes, rules, identities, or organizational forms change.
- **Rupture:** Key functions or identifiable relations no longer continue.
- **Insufficient evidence:** Surviving material does not allow a determination.

“Insufficient evidence” must not be forced into either persistence or rupture.

## 6. Research Design and Falsifiability

For each case:
1. Specify the network, time window, region, and alleged disruption.
2. Identify observable nodes, relations, and functions before and after the disruption.
3. Record source provenance and preservation bias.
4. Compare factors such as central control, local autonomy, route redundancy, and trust institutions.
5. Provide at least one competing explanation.
6. State what evidence would weaken or falsify the current hypothesis.

If a network collapses just as rapidly as its political center, or its continuity depends entirely on another powerful center, this may weaken a DSON explanation for that case.

## 7. Limitations

- Decentralized networks are not necessarily more efficient or stable than centralized institutions.
- High connectivity can accelerate transmission and the spread of crises.
- Hubs may improve efficiency while creating single points of failure.
- Network visibility is shaped by source survival, literate groups, and archaeological sampling.
- Similar names, religious symbols, or material culture do not alone prove network connections.
- Modern network-science terminology must not be projected onto ancient societies without careful translation and qualification.

## 8. Relationship to TCHS

TCHS provides chronological slices and a multi-scalar comparative structure. DSON provides network variables for testing explanations. Any DSON conclusion must return to identifiable people, places, events, and sources within the TCHS slices.
